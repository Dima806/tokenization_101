The model does not read the letters in a word. It reads tokens, and a token is a chunk of characters that a tokenizer learned from a large amount of ordinary text. The tokenizer looks at the text, counts the pairs of symbols that sit next to each other, and merges the most common pair into a new symbol. It repeats this simple step again and again, and the common chunks of the language slowly turn into single tokens. The word the becomes a token, the ending ing becomes a token, and a space before a common word becomes a token as well. Because the model only ever sees these tokens, it cannot see the letters hiding quietly inside them.
People learn to read slowly, one letter at a time, and then almost all at once the letters seem to disappear and only the meaning stays behind. A fluent reader no longer notices the separate marks on the page; whole words arrive as single familiar shapes, and the eye jumps ahead to the end of the sentence before the mouth could ever say it. We forget how strange this is until we meet a language we cannot read, where every shape is a small puzzle and nothing arrives as a whole.

The streets are quiet, the light is thin and grey, and the smell of baking drifts out of a small shop on the corner. You do not share a language with the person behind the counter, so you point and smile, and somehow that is enough. A good morning needs very few words.
Good work is mostly patience. You try a thing, you watch it fail, you change one small part, and then you try the whole thing again. Nothing clever usually happens; the clever part is simply not giving up when the first attempt falls apart in your hands. Over time the small changes add up, the rough edges wear smooth, and a thing that once looked impossible begins to feel ordinary and even easy.
Language is older than writing, and writing is younger than we tend to think. For most of human history people carried their stories in memory and passed them on by mouth, around a fire, in a field, on a long walk home. The marks we make on paper are only a recent trick for holding those sounds still, so that a voice can travel across years and miles and reach a reader who was never there.
When you understand tokens, a whole list of strange machine failures stops looking like magic. A model that cannot count the letters in a word, that stumbles over long numbers, or that charges far more for one language than another, is not being lazy or stupid. It is simply working with the chunks it was handed, and those chunks were shaped by the text it happened to learn from, long before it ever met your question.

When you understand tokens, a whole list of strange machine failures stops looking like magic. A model that cannot count the letters in a word, that stumbles over long numbers, or that charges far more for one language than another, is not being lazy or stupid. The model does not read the letters in a word. It reads tokens, and a token is a chunk of characters that a tokenizer learned from a large amount of ordinary text. The tokenizer looks at the text, counts the pairs of symbols that sit next to each other, and merges the most common pair into a new symbol. It repeats this simple step again and again, and the common chunks of the language slowly turn into single tokens. The word the becomes a token, the ending ing becomes a token, and a space before a common word becomes a token as well. Because the model only ever sees these tokens, it cannot see the letters hiding quietly inside them.

On a cold morning in an unfamiliar city, a cup of coffee and a warm piece of bread can feel like the kindest thing in the world. The streets are quiet, the light is thin and grey, and the smell of baking drifts out of a small shop on the corner. You do not share a language with the person behind the counter, so you point and smile, and somehow that is enough. A good morning needs very few words.
Good work is mostly patience. You try a thing, you watch it fail, you change one small part, and then you try the whole thing again. Nothing clever usually happens; the clever part is simply not giving up when the first attempt falls apart in your hands. Over time the small changes add up, the rough edges wear smooth, and a thing that once looked impossible begins to feel ordinary and even easy.
Language is older than writing, and writing is younger than we tend to think. For most of human history people carried their stories in memory and passed them on by mouth, around a fire, in a field, on a long walk home. The marks we make on paper are only a recent trick for holding those sounds still, so that a voice can travel across years and miles and reach a reader who was never there.
When you understand tokens, a whole list of strange machine failures stops looking like magic. A model that cannot count the letters in a word, that stumbles over long numbers, or that charges far more for one language than another, is not being lazy or stupid. It is simply working with the chunks it was handed, and those chunks were shaped by the text it happened to learn from, long before it ever met your question.
The model does not read the letters in a word. It reads tokens, and a token is a chunk of characters that a tokenizer learned from a large amount of ordinary text. The tokenizer looks at the text, counts the pairs of symbols that sit next to each other, and merges the most common pair into a new symbol. It repeats this simple step again and again, and the common chunks of the language slowly turn into single tokens. The word the becomes a token, the ending ing becomes a token, and a space before a common word becomes a token as well. Because the model only ever sees these tokens, it cannot see the letters hiding quietly inside them.

Good work is mostly patience. You try a thing, you watch it fail, you change one small part, and then you try the whole thing again. Nothing clever usually happens; the clever part is simply not giving up when the first attempt falls apart in your hands. Over time the small changes add up, the rough edges wear smooth, and a thing that once looked impossible begins to feel ordinary and even easy.
Language is older than writing, and writing is younger than we tend to think. For most of human history people carried their stories in memory and passed them on by mouth, around a fire, in a field, on a long walk home. The marks we make on paper are only a recent trick for holding those sounds still, so that a voice can travel across years and miles and reach a reader who was never there.
When you understand tokens, a whole list of strange machine failures stops looking like magic. A model that cannot count the letters in a word, that stumbles over long numbers, or that charges far more for one language than another, is not being lazy or stupid. It is simply working with the chunks it was handed, and those chunks were shaped by the text it happened to learn from, long before it ever met your question.
The model does not read the letters in a word. It reads tokens, and a token is a chunk of characters that a tokenizer learned from a large amount of ordinary text. The tokenizer looks at the text, counts the pairs of symbols that sit next to each other, and merges the most common pair into a new symbol. It repeats this simple step again and again, and the common chunks of the language slowly turn into single tokens. The word the becomes a token, the ending ing becomes a token, and a space before a common word becomes a token as well. Because the model only ever sees these tokens, it cannot see the letters hiding quietly inside them.
People learn to read slowly, one letter at a time, and then almost all at once the letters seem to disappear and only the meaning stays behind. A fluent reader no longer notices the separate marks on the page; whole words arrive as single familiar shapes, and the eye jumps ahead to the end of the sentence before the mouth could ever say it. Good work is mostly patience. You try a thing, you watch it fail, you change one small part, and then you try the whole thing again. Nothing clever usually happens; the clever part is simply not giving up when the first attempt falls apart in your hands. Over time the small changes add up, the rough edges wear smooth, and a thing that once looked impossible begins to feel ordinary and even easy.
Language is older than writing, and writing is younger than we tend to think. For most of human history people carried their stories in memory and passed them on by mouth, around a fire, in a field, on a long walk home. The marks we make on paper are only a recent trick for holding those sounds still, so that a voice can travel across years and miles and reach a reader who was never there.
When you understand tokens, a whole list of strange machine failures stops looking like magic. A model that cannot count the letters in a word, that stumbles over long numbers, or that charges far more for one language than another, is not being lazy or stupid. It is simply working with the chunks it was handed, and those chunks were shaped by the text it happened to learn from, long before it ever met your question.
The model does not read the letters in a word. It reads tokens, and a token is a chunk of characters that a tokenizer learned from a large amount of ordinary text. The tokenizer looks at the text, counts the pairs of symbols that sit next to each other, and merges the most common pair into a new symbol. It repeats this simple step again and again, and the common chunks of the language slowly turn into single tokens. The word the becomes a token, the ending ing becomes a token, and a space before a common word becomes a token as well. Because the model only ever sees these tokens, it cannot see the letters hiding quietly inside them.
People learn to read slowly, one letter at a time, and then almost all at once the letters seem to disappear and only the meaning stays behind. A fluent reader no longer notices the separate marks on the page; whole words arrive as single familiar shapes, and the eye jumps ahead to the end of the sentence before the mouth could ever say it. We forget how strange this is until we meet a language we cannot read, where every shape is a small puzzle and nothing arrives as a whole.
